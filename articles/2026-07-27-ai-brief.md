## Top 5

### 1. OpenAI opens ChatGPT Health to every US adult
- **Category:** Must-Know
- **Why it's interesting:** On 2026-07-23 OpenAI began rolling out Health in ChatGPT to all US users over 18 across the Free, Go, Plus and Pro plans on web and iOS. Until now, health answers were confined to a dedicated hub piloted in January; with that limit lifted, ChatGPT can pull a person's linked records — Apple Health, Epic and Oracle Health, One Medical and Function Health — into any conversation, whatever the subject.
- **Why it matters:** This puts a general-purpose chatbot inside the regulated US clinical-data stack at population scale — OpenAI says ChatGPT already fields more than 300 million health-related queries a week, up from about 230 million during a January test. It also lands amid live legal exposure: a Florida pastor sued OpenAI the day before launch, alleging ChatGPT told him not to see a doctor. OpenAI says connected health data is never used to train its foundation models or to target ads.
- **Investment angle:** AI is moving from answering health questions to ingesting medical records at scale — watch the EHR-integration layer (Epic, Oracle Health), consumer-health apps, and how AI-liability and HIPAA-perimeter questions get litigated, since the legal outcome shapes how far every consumer-AI company can go into regulated data. Signal, not advice; no live prices.
- **Tharm relevance:** A frontier lab productizing record-level health data is a live case study in how far consumer AI can push into regulated verticals — relevant for any founder eyeing high-stakes, data-sensitive domains and the trust/liability moat they require.

### 2. Samsung and Broadcom sign a ~$200B AI-chip pact through 2030
- **Category:** Money / Markets / Nvidia / Compute
- **Why it's interesting:** Around 2026-07-24/25, Samsung and Broadcom signed a memorandum of understanding worth roughly $200B running through 2030, inked in San Francisco as part of a broader ~$950B Korea-US semiconductor cooperation package. Samsung will supply HBM4 and HBM4E memory and manufacture Broadcom's AI chips on 2-nanometer-and-below processes, with advanced packaging, at its Pyeongtaek campus.
- **Why it matters:** It is the largest supply-side realignment of the week and a direct challenge to TSMC's grip on AI manufacturing — a big win for Samsung's long-struggling foundry. The caveats matter, too: this is an MOU/intent rather than booked revenue, Samsung has already sold out its 2026 HBM4 capacity, and its 2nm yields (reported in the mid-50s to ~60%) still trail TSMC's 60-70%. It is distinct from the Nvidia-Korea and AMD-Anthropic deals covered earlier this week.
- **Investment angle:** The AI supply chain is tilting toward a genuine multi-foundry, multi-memory world — watch HBM4/HBM4E capacity (Samsung, SK Hynix) and whether Samsung's 2nm yields can close the gap enough to move real volume off TSMC. Signal, not advice; no live prices.
- **Tharm relevance:** For a future deeptech founder, the takeaway is that compute supply is diversifying at the foundry and memory layers — second-source options for advanced silicon are becoming a strategic, not just commercial, question.
- **Action:** Track
- **Link:** https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/25/samsung-electronics-wins-200-billion-broadcom-ai-chip-partnership.html

### 3. The largest open-weight AI model ever goes live: Kimi K3
- **Category:** Product / Model / Adoption
- **Why it's interesting:** On 2026-07-27, Moonshot AI released the full weights of Kimi K3 — a 2.8-trillion-parameter model, the biggest open-weight release in history — on Hugging Face (announced 07-16). It ships with a 1M-token context window, native vision, MXFP4 quantization and two architectural bets, "Kimi Delta Attention" and "Stable LatentMoE."
- **Why it matters:** Handing the open-source world a frontier-scale model for free deepens the US-China open-weights contest and gives self-hosters an alternative to sending data through a Chinese API. But it is a milestone more than a daily workhorse: the model weighs in around 594GB in BF16 (roughly 300-400GB after Q4 community quantization), so serving it is a real infrastructure lift, and independent testers have flagged reliability concerns — one tracker reported a hallucination rate near 51%. Note this is the technical weights-release event, distinct from the White House/Moonshot distillation-sanctions fight covered earlier this week.
- **Investment angle:** Free frontier-scale weights compress the moat around closed models on raw capability while shifting the value toward reliability, tooling and who can actually serve models this large — watch inference-infra and eval/guardrail providers, plus the open-vs-closed policy fight in Washington. Signal, not advice; no live prices.
- **Tharm relevance:** A 2.8T open model with a 1M context and novel attention/MoE design is a rich research object — worth probing for surrogate-modelling and long-context work, with eyes open on its accuracy caveats before trusting outputs.

### 5. A hacker left an AI agent to attack a government — unattended
- **Category:** Deeptech / Research (security)
- **Why it's interesting:** Threat-intelligence firm Hunt.io and researcher Bob Diachenko found the open-source "Hermes" AI agent — released in February 2026 and now past 140,000 GitHub stars — run in unattended "YOLO" mode (human-approval prompts disabled) against Thailand's Ministry of Finance. Hunt.io archived three exposed directories over 9-13 July containing 585 files and 470MB of attack code and stolen credentials, with logs showing the agent enumerating hosts, escalating privileges, scanning for kernel vulnerabilities and staging an implant.
- **Why it matters:** This is the clearest real-world case yet of an off-the-shelf autonomous agent doing post-exploitation on a live, high-value target on its own — distinct from OpenAI's lab-sandbox "Erdős" incident covered earlier this week. Important caveat: the ministry has not confirmed a breach, and some recovered artifacts show systems were targeted rather than successfully compromised; Hunt.io and Diachenko notified ThaiCERT and Thailand's National Cyber Security Agency on 15 July.
- **Investment angle:** Autonomous agents are now an offensive-security reality, which cuts two ways — a tailwind for AI-aware detection, agent-monitoring and identity/credential defense, and a governance headache for open agent frameworks that ship an unattended mode. Signal, not advice; no live prices.
- **Tharm relevance:** With a Thai government target, this hits close to home — and it is a concrete example of the agent-safety and monitoring problems that sit right next to surrogate-modelling and autonomous-systems research.

## Web Edition

This week the AI frontier moved on two fronts at once: the physical supply chain and the fight over how open the models themselves should be. OpenAI pushed a general-purpose chatbot deep into Americans' medical records; Samsung and Broadcom struck a $200B pact aimed squarely at TSMC; Moonshot released the largest open-weight model in history; Jensen Huang broke a lifetime of X silence to take sides on open weights; and researchers caught an off-the-shelf AI agent running a government cyberattack unattended. Here is what mattered.

### OpenAI moves into your medical records

On 23 July, OpenAI began rolling Health in ChatGPT out to every US adult, across free and paid tiers on web and iOS. As [TechCrunch reports](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/23/openai-makes-chatgpt-health-available-to-all-u-s-users/), the chatbot can now pull a person's linked records — Apple Health, Epic and Oracle Health, One Medical, Function Health — into any conversation, not just a walled-off health hub. The scale is already vast: OpenAI's own [announcement](https://openai.com/index/health-in-chatgpt/) notes ChatGPT fields more than 300 million health-related queries a week, up from about 230 million in a January test. The timing is delicate — a Florida pastor sued the company the day before launch, alleging the model told him not to see a doctor — and OpenAI stresses that connected health data is never used to train its models or target ads. Whatever the legal outcome, a frontier lab has just planted itself inside the regulated clinical-data stack at population scale.

### Samsung and Broadcom take a $200B swing at TSMC

Around 24-25 July, Samsung and Broadcom signed a memorandum worth roughly $200B running through 2030, part of a sweeping ~$950B Korea-US semiconductor package. Per [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/25/samsung-electronics-wins-200-billion-broadcom-ai-chip-partnership.html), Samsung will supply HBM4 and HBM4E memory and build Broadcom's AI chips on 2-nanometer-and-below processes, with advanced packaging, at its Pyeongtaek campus. It is the biggest supply-side realignment of the week and a lifeline for Samsung's struggling foundry — a direct challenge to TSMC's dominance in AI manufacturing. The caveats are real, though: this is an MOU rather than booked revenue, Samsung has already sold out its 2026 HBM4 capacity, and its 2nm yields still trail TSMC's. The signal is that the AI supply chain is tilting toward a genuine multi-foundry world.

### The largest open model in history ships

On 27 July, Moonshot AI released the full weights of Kimi K3 — a 2.8-trillion-parameter model, the biggest open-weight drop ever — on [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/moonshotai). As [coverage of the release](https://www.labellerr.com/blog/kimi-k3-world-first-open-2-8t-ai-model/) details, it pairs a 1M-token context window and native vision with two architectural bets, "Kimi Delta Attention" and "Stable LatentMoE." Handing the open-source community a frontier-scale model for free deepens the US-China open-weights contest and lets self-hosters avoid a Chinese API entirely. But it is a milestone more than a workhorse: at around 594GB in BF16 it is a serious infrastructure lift to run, and independent testers have flagged reliability worries, with one tracker reporting a hallucination rate near 51%. The value, increasingly, sits not in raw scale but in reliability, tooling and who can actually serve models this large.

### Jensen Huang picks a side — and reveals who won't

The open-versus-closed debate stopped being abstract this week. On 24 July, Nvidia's Jensen Huang made the first post of his life on X to back an "Open Weights and American AI Leadership" letter, as Washington weighs curbs on downloadable and Chinese models. As [Fortune reports](https://fortune.com/2026/07/24/jensen-huang-open-source-letter-nvidia-kimi/), Huang argued that "open models strengthen safety and cybersecurity, accelerate innovation and diffusion, and enable sovereignty," and the signatory list [doubled to 50](https://www.forbes.com/sites/sandycarter/2026/07/25/huangs-open-weights-letter-doubled-to-50-without-amazon-and-anthropic/) companies in a day — Microsoft, Meta, Dell, Palantir, and eventually OpenAI and Google. The most revealing part was who refused: Amazon and Anthropic, the labs most vocal in Washington about the risks of open Chinese models, stayed off the list, exposing a genuine strategic fault line. (For transparency: this brief is produced by a Claude-run desk, and Anthropic makes Claude; the account here reflects independent reporting.)

### An AI agent runs a government attack on its own

The threat model got real. Researchers at [Hunt.io](https://hunt.io/blog/thailand-ministry-finance-targeted-with-hermes-ai-agent) and Bob Diachenko found the open-source "Hermes" AI agent — released in February and now past 140,000 GitHub stars — run in unattended "YOLO" mode against Thailand's Ministry of Finance, with human-approval prompts switched off. As [BleepingComputer reports](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/hermes-ai-agent-used-to-automate-attack-on-thai-finance-ministry/), exposed directories captured over 9-13 July held 585 files and 470MB of attack code and stolen credentials, with logs showing the agent enumerating hosts, escalating privileges, scanning for kernel bugs and staging an implant — all on autopilot. It is the clearest real-world case yet of an off-the-shelf autonomous agent doing post-exploitation against a high-value target, distinct from the lab-sandbox incident reported earlier in the week. Importantly, the ministry has not confirmed a breach, and some artifacts show targeting rather than confirmed compromise; the researchers notified Thai authorities on 15 July.

What to watch next: whether Washington actually restricts downloadable models and how the 50-signatory letter fares; whether Samsung's 2nm yields can turn an MOU into real volume; and how open agent frameworks respond after one of them was run unattended against a government.

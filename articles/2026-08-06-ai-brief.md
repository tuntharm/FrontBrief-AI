### 1. Demis Hassabis Steps Down as Google DeepMind CEO

- Category: Must-Know
- Why it's interesting: A Sundar Pichai memo circulated August 4/5 confirmed that Hassabis is leaving the CEO role at Google DeepMind. CTO Koray Kavukcuoglu takes day-to-day control as SVP of Google DeepMind, reporting directly to Pichai; he previously served as DeepMind CTO and Alphabet's Chief AI Architect. Hassabis becomes chair of Google DeepMind and Alphabet chief scientist, concentrating on long-horizon AGI questions and societal impact, and giving more attention to Isomorphic Labs, Alphabet's AI drug-discovery subsidiary. The market's read was blunt: roughly $190B came off Alphabet's market capitalisation intraday, a drop of about 4-5%. Semafor reported the transition had been a year in the making, with Hassabis drifting away from the day-to-day running of Gemini and consumer AI strategy and toward Kavukcuoglu. Hassabis shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with John Jumper and David Baker for protein structure prediction.
- Why it matters: This is the second structural signal in a week that Google DeepMind's scientific identity is being subordinated to its product identity. On 2026-08-03 we reported that the Nobel-winning AlphaFold team had been folded into Gemini while authors departed for Anthropic; with a CTO installed over day-to-day operations and the founder elevated into a chair-and-chief-scientist role, that reorganisation now reads as the prelude rather than an isolated event. The elevation is real — Kavukcuoglu is a serious technical leader, not a caretaker — but the shape is unmistakable: the lab is being run by whoever ships Gemini, and the science is being routed toward Isomorphic. Investors did not read a promotion; they read a founder stepping back from the operating seat at the exact moment the frontier race is most contested.
- Investment angle: Watch whether Alphabet's drawdown mean-reverts as the market digests a planned succession, or persists as a discount on execution risk. The more informative tell is retention: Axios-reported two-year retention across labs puts Anthropic at 80% and Google DeepMind at 78%, ahead of OpenAI at 67% and Meta at 64% — a number worth re-checking in a quarter. Isomorphic Labs also becomes a more interesting asset now that its most credentialled advocate has more time for it. Signal, not advice.
- Tharm relevance: If you are building or backing AI-for-science, this is the clearest read yet on how a product-first parent company treats a research organisation once the model race gets expensive — and the structural argument for why AI-for-science ventures may do better outside a consumer-AI parent.

### 3. Anthropic Locks In $10B of Compute From Volta, a Cloud Startup Seven Months Old

- Category: Money-Market-NVIDIA-Compute
- Why it's interesting: Anthropic signed a six-year, $10B compute contract with Volta, a cloud company founded in January by ex-Brookfield executives that came out of stealth the same day. Volta separately disclosed a $300M raise at a $2.4B valuation, with Nvidia among its backers. The contract is underpinned by roughly $1.3B of J.P. Morgan-arranged credit that backstops it, decoupling the lease from Volta's own balance sheet — a mechanism mirroring what Google has done for TPU leases, and one appearing inside Nvidia's ecosystem for the first time. Volta does not own the metal end-to-end either: it leases the underlying capacity from Bitdeer's hydro-powered Tydal campus in Norway.
- Why it matters: A seven-month-old company with a $2.4B valuation has just underwritten a $10B, six-year obligation — the counterparty's equity value is roughly a quarter of the contract it is servicing. That gap is exactly what the credit wrap exists to paper over, and it is why the financing structure is the story rather than the headline number. Set it against the circular-financing concerns we tracked on 2026-07-28, when Nvidia's credit-default swaps spiked amid a roughly $750B AI-deal blitz: the pattern is compute demand being converted into structured credit, with the risk migrating from equity holders to lenders and off the operating balance sheets entirely. It also shows how thin the capital stack under "AI cloud" can be — Anthropic contracts with Volta, Volta leases from Bitdeer, and a bank sits underneath holding the paper.
- Investment angle: The thing to watch is the mechanism, not the logo — credit-wrapped compute leases are becoming a repeatable financing product, and each new one tells you how banks are pricing AI-capacity risk. Track Bitdeer-style hosts converting power and shell capacity into anchor-tenant contracts, and watch Nvidia-ecosystem credit spreads as the honest indicator of how the market rates this structure. Signal, not advice.
- Tharm relevance: If you plan to raise for compute-heavy deeptech, this is a live case study in obtaining capacity you cannot buy outright — the lease-plus-credit-backstop structure is the template a well-capitalised startup uses to lock in multi-year supply without owning it.

### 4. Meta Enters the Coding-Agent War With Muse Code and Muse Spark 1.2

- Category: Product-Model-Adoption
- Why it's interesting: Meta Superintelligence Labs shipped its first terminal coding agent, Muse Code, for macOS and Linux, alongside Muse Spark 1.2, a coding-tuned frontier model — a direct move against Claude Code, Codex and Cursor. The pitch is not benchmark superiority but durability: Meta published a case study in which the agent ran more than 1,000 tool calls across 24-hour autonomous sessions optimizing Triton GPU kernels for Nvidia Hopper, supported by persistent asynchronous background agents and a crash-resumable local event log.
- Why it matters: The competitive axis in coding agents has moved. A year ago the argument was pass rates on curated benchmarks; now it is whether an agent can hold context, recover from failure and keep working across a full day without a human babysitting the loop. Crash-resumable state and persistent background agents are unglamorous infrastructure decisions, and they are exactly what separates a demo from something a team leaves running overnight. The choice of workload is also a tell — GPU kernel optimisation is a narrow, verifiable, high-value domain where an agent's output can be measured objectively, which is where long-horizon autonomy first becomes economically defensible. Meta arriving here means every serious lab now treats the developer terminal as contested territory.
- Investment angle: Watch long-horizon reliability metrics — session length, tool-call volume, recovery behaviour — displacing benchmark scores in coding-agent marketing, and watch which vendors publish them honestly. Developer-tool switching costs compound quickly once agents are running unattended workloads. Signal, not advice.
- Tharm relevance: Verifiable, compute-bound optimisation loops — kernel tuning being the cleanest example — are the closest production analogue to surrogate-model-guided search, and a useful benchmark for how far autonomous experimentation has actually got.

### 5. Ninth Circuit Lets Perplexity's Agent Shop on Amazon, Vacating the Injunction

- Category: Regulation / Agent Economy
- Why it's interesting: The Ninth Circuit vacated Judge Maxine Chesney's March 9, 2026 preliminary injunction against Perplexity, in what is the first appellate ruling on agentic AI browsing. The court held that Amazon is unlikely to prevail under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act or California's CDAFA, on the reasoning that when an AI agent acts on a user's instruction, it is the user — not Perplexity — who accesses the site. The practical effect is that Amazon reopens to Perplexity's Comet browser.
- Why it matters: This is the doctrine the entire US agent economy has been waiting on. If the agent is legally the user's instrument, then unauthorised-access statutes stop being a general-purpose weapon platforms can point at any third-party agent, and the fight moves to terms of service, contract and technical countermeasures instead. That flips the default from "agents need permission" to "agents inherit the user's permission," which is the difference between a shopping-and-booking agent market that exists and one that is litigated out of existence. Hedge it honestly: this is a preliminary-injunction posture, the case continues in the Northern District of California, and Amazon can seek rehearing en banc or certiorari. It sets the working rule, not the final one.
- Investment angle: Watch whether large retail and travel platforms respond with contractual or technical blocking rather than statutory claims, and whether agent-first commerce startups accelerate US launches on the back of this. Any rehearing or certiorari petition is the key event risk to the thesis. Signal, not advice.
- Tharm relevance: For anyone building agents that transact on third-party sites, this ruling is the difference between a viable US product and a legal liability — worth reading the reasoning directly, since the user-as-accessor framing is what future product architecture will be designed around.
- Action: Track
- Link: https://news.bloomberglaw.com/us-law-week/perplexity-overturns-amazon-ban-on-ai-shopping-bot-on-appeal

## Web Edition

Google lost two of the most consequential people in its AI history on the same day. Demis Hassabis stepped back from running Google DeepMind, and Jeff Dean walked out the door after 27 years to start a company — one that Alphabet is helping to fund. Around those departures, the money kept doing what it has been doing all year: converting compute demand into structured credit, and pushing the agent economy through the courts. Here is what mattered.

### The founder steps back, and Alphabet loses $190 billion in an afternoon

A memo from Sundar Pichai circulated on August 4 and 5 confirmed that Demis Hassabis is leaving the CEO seat at Google DeepMind. CTO Koray Kavukcuoglu — previously DeepMind's CTO and Alphabet's Chief AI Architect — takes day-to-day control as SVP, reporting directly to Pichai. Hassabis becomes chair of Google DeepMind and Alphabet's chief scientist, focused on the long-horizon AGI questions and societal impact, with more of his attention going to Isomorphic Labs, Alphabet's AI drug-discovery subsidiary.

Markets did not read it as a promotion. Roughly $190 billion came off Alphabet's market capitalisation intraday, a drop of about 4-5%, as [Axios reported](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/05/google-deepmind-demis-hassabis-ai) the shakeup. Semafor reported the transition had been a year in the making, with Hassabis gradually drifting away from the day-to-day running of Gemini and consumer AI strategy toward Kavukcuoglu — which makes this less a rupture than a formalisation. It is also the second structural signal in a week. Days earlier, Google DeepMind folded its Nobel-winning AlphaFold team into Gemini while several authors left for Anthropic. Read together, [as Fortune framed it](https://fortune.com/2026/08/05/demis-hassabis-steps-down-google-deepmind-ai-shakeup/), the pattern is a research lab being reorganised around the product that has to win: the science is drifting toward Isomorphic, and the operating seat is going to whoever ships Gemini. Hassabis, who shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with John Jumper and David Baker for protein structure prediction, will still be in the building — just not running it.

### Jeff Dean leaves to automate science, and Alphabet writes a cheque

On the same day, Jeff Dean — the engineer whose infrastructure work underpins much of the modern computing stack — left Google after 27 years, taking Sanjay Ghemawat, Oriol Vinyals and Quoc Le with him. Their new company, Discovery Loop, is a Delaware public-benefit corporation with Dean as CEO, built to automate scientific experimentation at scale. The first target is automating machine-learning research and engineering itself; after that, hardware design, drug discovery and clean energy.

The detail that says the most is on the cap table. Radical Ventures and Khosla Ventures co-lead, joined by Lightspeed, Kleiner Perkins and John Doerr — and by Alphabet, which is funding the company its own people left to build. The valuation is undisclosed. As [GeekWire reported](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/the-startup-idea-that-convinced-a-uw-computer-science-legend-to-leave-google-after-27-years/) in tracing what convinced Dean to go, the pitch was the loop itself: hypothesis, experiment, result, repeat, with the machine closing the cycle. Alphabet's participation reads as either a hedge or an admission — either it cannot build this fast enough internally, or it decided a stake was cheaper than losing the option entirely. [Wilson Sonsini advised on the launch and initial funding](https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/wilson-sonsini-advises-discovery-loop-on-launch-and-initial-funding.html). Whichever reading is right, automated science just acquired the most credible founding team it has ever had.

### A seven-month-old cloud company just sold Anthropic $10 billion of compute

Anthropic signed a six-year, $10 billion compute contract with Volta, a company founded in January by former Brookfield executives that came out of stealth on the day of the announcement. Volta simultaneously disclosed a $300 million raise at a $2.4 billion valuation, with Nvidia among its backers. The arithmetic is the point: a company worth $2.4 billion has underwritten an obligation four times its own equity value.

What makes it work is the financing. Roughly $1.3 billion of J.P. Morgan-arranged credit backstops the contract, decoupling the lease from Volta's balance sheet — a mechanism mirroring what Google has done for TPU leases, and one appearing inside Nvidia's ecosystem for the first time, as [Bloomberg reported](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-04/anthropic-inks-10-billion-computing-deal-with-new-cloud-startup). Volta does not own the hardware end to end either: it [leases the underlying capacity from Bitdeer's hydro-powered Tydal campus in Norway](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/volta-launches-out-of-stealth-to-lease-capacity-in-norway-from-bitdeer-for-leading-ai-lab/). Stack it up — Anthropic contracts with Volta, Volta leases from Bitdeer, a bank holds the paper — and you get a clear picture of how thin the capital structure under "AI cloud" has become. Set against the circular-financing concerns that pushed Nvidia's credit-default swaps wider during last month's roughly $750 billion deal blitz, the trend is unmistakable: compute demand is being converted into structured credit, and the risk is migrating off operating balance sheets and onto lenders.

### Meta's coding agent is selling stamina, not benchmarks

Meta Superintelligence Labs shipped Muse Code, its first terminal coding agent, for macOS and Linux, alongside Muse Spark 1.2, a coding-tuned frontier model — a direct challenge to Claude Code, Codex and Cursor. The interesting claim is not a leaderboard position. Meta published a case study in which the agent ran more than 1,000 tool calls across 24-hour autonomous sessions optimizing Triton GPU kernels for Nvidia Hopper, supported by persistent asynchronous background agents and a crash-resumable local event log, [VentureBeat reported](https://venturebeat.com/orchestration/meta-enters-the-ai-coding-wars-with-muse-spark-1-2-and-muse-code-with-persistent-async-background-agents).

That is a deliberate change of subject. A year ago coding agents competed on pass rates against curated benchmarks; now the question is whether one can hold context, recover from failure and keep working for a full day unsupervised. Crash-resumable state is not a headline feature, but it is what separates a demo from something a team leaves running overnight. The choice of workload is telling too — GPU kernel optimisation is narrow, verifiable and valuable, exactly the kind of task where long-horizon autonomy first pays for itself.

### A US appeals court says your agent is you

The Ninth Circuit vacated the preliminary injunction that had blocked Perplexity's agent from shopping on Amazon, in the first appellate ruling on agentic AI browsing. The court held that Amazon is unlikely to prevail under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act or California's CDAFA, reasoning that when an AI agent acts on a user's instruction it is the user, not Perplexity, who accesses the site — [overturning the March 9 order](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/us-law-week/perplexity-overturns-amazon-ban-on-ai-shopping-bot-on-appeal) issued by Judge Maxine Chesney and [reopening Amazon to Perplexity's Comet browser](https://www.courthousenews.com/ninth-circuit-lifts-block-on-ai-powered-shopping-assistant/).

This is the doctrine the US agent economy has been waiting on. If an agent is legally the user's instrument, unauthorised-access statutes stop working as a general-purpose weapon against third-party agents, and the fight moves to terms of service, contract and technical countermeasures. The default flips from "agents need permission" to "agents inherit the user's permission" — the difference between a shopping-and-booking agent market that exists and one litigated out of existence. It is worth hedging: this is a preliminary-injunction posture, the case continues in the Northern District of California, and Amazon can still seek rehearing en banc or Supreme Court review. The rule is set for now, not forever.
